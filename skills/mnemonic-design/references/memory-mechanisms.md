# Memory Mechanisms and Design Implications

This is an operational design reference, not a clinical model.

## Encoding
Information is processed into a form that can later support retrieval. Meaningful organization and prior-knowledge connections often help; arbitrary details may need distinctive associations.
**Implication:** establish meaning first; add an artificial cue only for what remains arbitrary or difficult.

## Retrieval cues
A cue supports access when it is associated with the target and available at test time.
**Implication:** make cues explicit, distinctive, and appropriate to the future retrieval context. Practice the direction required later.

## Distinctiveness
Distinctive cues can reduce confusion, but novelty alone is insufficient: a memorable weakly mapped image may retrieve the wrong answer.
**Implication:** encode the feature that differentiates the target from similar items.

## Elaboration and association
Connecting new material to known knowledge can create retrieval routes if the connection is accurate.
**Implication:** state and verify the mapping.

## Chunking
Meaningful grouping can reduce the number of separate units handled.
**Implication:** group by meaning, not arbitrary group size; preserve internal order.

## Spatial organization
A stable route can provide ordered retrieval cues.
**Implication:** use distinct loci, one dominant image per locus, and rehearse the required direction.

## Retrieval practice
Attempting recall supports later accessibility in many contexts. Failed retrieval should be followed by feedback and correction.
**Implication:** require an answer before revealing it; distinguish correct, partial, and incorrect responses.

## Distributed practice
Practice distributed over time often supports longer-term retention better than massed practice.
**Implication:** schedule later retrieval and adjust intervals to performance and retention horizon.

## Distinctions
Recognition ≠ free recall. Familiarity ≠ accurate retrieval. Vividness ≠ retention. Remembering steps ≠ performing a procedure. Confidence ≠ correctness.

## Evidence boundaries
Treat these as broad design principles, not deterministic laws. Effects depend on prior knowledge, material, task, practice, feedback, and context.
