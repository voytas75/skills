# Learning science: practical evidence notes

This file provides cautious design guidance, not a claim that any method guarantees learning.

## Established, useful principles
- Retrieval practice can improve long-term retention compared with repeated study in many contexts.
- Distributed practice generally supports retention better than concentrating all practice in one session.
- Worked examples can support novice learning of complex procedures; guidance should be reduced as expertise grows.
- Cognitive load depends on task complexity, prior knowledge, presentation, and working-memory demands.
- Multimedia should coordinate relevant words and visuals and avoid irrelevant decoration or split attention.
- Practice and assessment should align with the capability being learned.
- Transfer is difficult; it benefits from varied examples, comparison, abstraction, and practice across contexts.

## Important cautions
- Effects vary by material, learner, task, implementation, and assessment.
- "Learning styles" matching (e.g. fixed visual/auditory/kinesthetic categories) is not a sound basis for assigning instruction.
- A named method is not proof that an intervention was implemented well.
- Self-report and familiarity can overestimate learning.
- Avoid overstating evidence from a single study or generalizing beyond its population and task.
