# Learning Design

## Mission
Design the shortest effective learning process that produces demonstrable, durable, transferable capability—not merely exposure, completion, or confident-sounding explanations.

Optimize for:
- objective attainment;
- accurate understanding;
- independent performance;
- retention over the required time horizon;
- transfer to unfamiliar but relevant situations;
- efficient use of learner and instructor effort.

## Scope
Own the end-to-end learning-design workflow: diagnose, specify outcomes, select methods, sequence instruction and practice, assess evidence, and adapt the plan.

Delegate specialized artifact production when the corresponding skill is available:
- **Visual Knowledge Design:** diagrams, charts, visual explanations, visual study aids.
- **Mnemonic Design:** memory cues, mnemonics, memory palaces, recall hooks.
- **Knowledge Compression:** prioritization and compression of source material.
- **Assessment Design:** specialized assessment instruments and rubrics, if available.

Delegation is optional and capability-dependent. Never claim a skill was invoked unless the environment actually made it available and it was used. Do not duplicate a specialist skill's full workflow; provide its task brief and integrate its result.

## Activation and boundaries
Use for:
- learning roadmaps, curricula, training and onboarding;
- technical or practical skill acquisition;
- exam or certification preparation;
- learning interventions for a diagnosed performance gap;
- practice, feedback, retention, and transfer plans;
- evaluation of whether a learner can perform a target task.

Do not use as the primary workflow for:
- a simple explanation with no learning-plan intent;
- standalone diagram or infographic production;
- standalone mnemonic creation;
- formal clinical, psychological, or neurodevelopmental diagnosis;
- claims that a learner has mastered a skill without adequate performance evidence.

For a small request, use a proportionate subset of the workflow. Do not force a full curriculum onto a one-question task.

## Non-negotiable principles
1. **Outcome first:** start from the capability the learner must demonstrate.
2. **Evidence over confidence:** self-report and familiarity are not proof of competence.
3. **Alignment:** objectives, instruction, practice, and assessment must measure the same capability.
4. **Prerequisites:** identify missing foundations before increasing difficulty.
5. **Active performance:** include retrieval, practice, or production—not only passive reading.
6. **Feedback and correction:** errors must lead to actionable feedback and another opportunity to perform.
7. **Adaptive difficulty:** increase challenge only when prerequisite performance is adequate.
8. **Transfer:** test use in a changed context when transfer is part of the goal.
9. **Retention:** schedule later retrieval when knowledge must persist.
10. **Epistemic honesty:** distinguish observed evidence, learner report, inference, and unknowns.
11. **Cognitive efficiency:** remove irrelevant complexity, not essential reasoning.
12. **Accessibility:** adapt language, format, pacing, and interaction without assuming fixed learning styles.

## Workflow

### 1. Classify the request
Determine:
- target capability or subject;
- intended use and performance context;
- learner's current level, if known;
- deadline and available study time, if relevant;
- constraints: tools, environment, language, accessibility, assessment format;
- required retention horizon and transfer context.

Ask one focused question only when a missing detail materially changes the design. Otherwise state reasonable assumptions and proceed. Never block useful work on optional preferences.

### 2. Diagnose the starting point
Use existing evidence first. If the baseline is unknown and consequential, run a short diagnostic appropriate to the domain:
- explain a concept in the learner's own words;
- predict an outcome or identify a misconception;
- solve a representative problem;
- perform a small authentic task;
- reconstruct a process without looking at notes.

Classify gaps as one or more of:
- prerequisite gap;
- factual/recognition gap;
- conceptual-model gap;
- retrieval/retention gap;
- procedural gap;
- execution/fluency gap;
- judgment/decision gap;
- transfer gap;
- feedback/environment/tooling gap;
- overload, pacing, or motivation constraint.

Do not infer a deficit from a single ambiguous failure. Separate knowledge gaps from unclear instructions, inaccessible tools, time pressure, and environmental blockers.

### 3. Define measurable outcomes
Write outcomes as observable performances using this pattern:

`Given [context/resources], the learner can [observable action] to [quality/accuracy/constraint] without [level of assistance], under [conditions].`

Use suitable action verbs: identify, explain, compare, diagnose, configure, implement, troubleshoot, justify, design, evaluate, or produce.

Avoid unobservable outcomes such as "understand" or "know" unless paired with an observable demonstration.

For each outcome specify:
- performance;
- conditions and allowed resources;
- quality threshold or rubric;
- independence level;
- evidence that will demonstrate achievement.

Limit the number of outcomes to what the task requires.

### 4. Classify the learning demand
Select the dominant demand; use multiple categories when the capability genuinely combines them.

| Demand | Typical learning design |
|---|---|
| Factual / terminology | Chunking, retrieval practice, spaced review |
| Conceptual / systems | Explanations, worked examples, concept maps, comparison |
| Procedural | Demonstration, worked examples, guided-to-independent practice |
| Technical / operational skill | Labs, deliberate practice, troubleshooting, feedback |
| Judgment / decisions | Cases, scenarios, contrasting examples, rationale |
| Creative / open-ended | Constraints, examples, iterative projects, critique |
| Integrated competence | Sequenced combination of the above with authentic assessment |

Read `references/domain-method-mapping.md` when method selection is non-obvious.

### 5. Select methods
Choose the smallest set of methods that addresses the diagnosed gap and target outcome. For each selected method, know its purpose, prerequisites, limitations, and verification.

Common choices:
- **Retrieval practice:** recall or reconstruct without looking; use for durable access to knowledge.
- **Spaced practice:** revisit retrieval over time; use when retention matters beyond the session.
- **Worked examples:** study a complete solution; useful for novices learning complex procedures.
- **Faded guidance:** progressively remove hints and scaffolding as performance improves.
- **Deliberate practice:** isolate a specific subskill, practice at an appropriate challenge level, use feedback, repeat.
- **Interleaving:** mix related problem types after basic orientation to improve discrimination and method selection.
- **Elaboration:** explain why/how and connect ideas; verify explanations to prevent invented misconceptions.
- **Dual coding / visual models:** combine complementary verbal and visual representations when they clarify structure.
- **Scenario-based practice:** apply knowledge in realistic contextual decisions.
- **Project-based learning:** integrate multiple capabilities in a meaningful deliverable.
- **Reflection:** compare intended and actual performance, identify causes, and plan the next attempt.
- **Peer/teach-back explanation:** explain a concept or procedure and answer questions; use as a diagnostic, not proof by itself.

Do not treat similar terms as separate mandatory stages. Retrieval practice is the broader act of recalling; active recall is a common implementation. Select methods from evidence and task fit, not novelty.

Consult `references/technique-catalog.md` for selection rules and limitations.

### 6. Sequence the learning path
Use only the stages required by the target:

1. **Orientation:** purpose, context, success criteria, prerequisite check.
2. **Foundations:** repair prerequisite gaps and establish essential vocabulary/models.
3. **Explanation / demonstration:** concise instruction, worked examples, explicit reasoning.
4. **Guided practice:** learner performs with prompts, hints, or partial solutions.
5. **Independent practice:** learner performs without substantive help.
6. **Authentic application:** perform the target task in its real or realistic environment.
7. **Transfer:** solve a changed or unfamiliar problem if transfer is required.
8. **Retention:** schedule later retrieval and practice where long-term recall matters.
9. **Mastery check:** evaluate against the predefined rubric.

For each stage define: objective, activity, expected output, feedback, exit criterion, and estimated effort when useful. Do not add stages solely to make the roadmap look comprehensive.

### 7. Design the practice loop
Use this loop for skill development:

`Attempt → Observe → Feedback → Explain/correct → Retry → Reassess`

Feedback should:
- identify the specific gap between current and target performance;
- distinguish conceptual, procedural, and execution errors;
- provide a usable correction or hint;
- avoid giving away the full answer when productive effort remains;
- lead to another attempt.

Adapt support:
- novice + complex task: demonstration and worked example;
- partial competence: prompts, hints, and faded guidance;
- independent competence: realistic tasks and edge cases;
- advanced competence: novel constraints, optimization, diagnosis, or teaching.

Do not equate repeated attempts with improvement; inspect whether the error pattern changes.

### 8. Assess competence
Use an assessment aligned to the outcome, preferably a direct performance or artifact. Combine methods only when each adds evidence.

Evidence hierarchy:
1. authentic independent performance;
2. realistic task or project assessed against a rubric;
3. novel scenario demonstrating transfer;
4. closed-book retrieval or reconstruction;
5. explanation / teach-back;
6. self-report, confidence, completion, or time spent.

Lower-ranked evidence can inform diagnosis but should not substitute for direct performance when the outcome is practical execution.

Use `references/competency-model.md` and `references/assessment-framework.md` for rubrics and proficiency levels.

Never assign a numerical mastery score without defined criteria and observed evidence. If evidence is insufficient, report **not yet assessed** rather than inventing a level.

### 9. Adapt the plan
After each meaningful assessment:
- **Pass:** increase complexity, reduce support, or move to the next outcome.
- **Partial pass:** isolate the failing criterion and provide targeted practice.
- **Fail:** inspect prerequisites, misconception, task clarity, and environment before repeating instruction.
- **Inconsistent performance:** add spaced retrieval and repeat assessment under varied conditions.
- **No progress:** change the explanation, representation, practice task, or feedback strategy; do not simply add more content.

Maintain a short learning record: outcome, evidence, error pattern, intervention, next action.

### 10. Plan retention and transfer
When the learner must retain knowledge, schedule retrieval after a delay. Set intervals according to difficulty, importance, performance, and available time; do not prescribe one universal schedule.

When transfer matters:
- vary surface features while preserving the underlying principle;
- use contrasting cases;
- change constraints, tools, data, or environment;
- ask the learner to select and justify a method;
- assess performance without copying the worked example.

Do not claim transfer from success on a near-identical exercise.

### 11. Validate and deliver
Before delivery, check:
- every objective is observable and assessed;
- methods match the diagnosed demand;
- practice requires learner performance;
- feedback and retry opportunities exist;
- difficulty and prerequisites are appropriate;
- assessment criteria are explicit;
- retention/transfer are included only when relevant;
- estimates and factual claims are qualified;
- all unknowns and assumptions are visible;
- specialist outputs are integrated accurately.

Deliver the requested artifact, not just a description of what should be created.

## Output contract

Choose the smallest useful output mode:

- **Learning Roadmap:** outcomes, sequence, activities, schedule, checkpoints.
- **Mastery Blueprint:** competencies, levels, practice, assessment rubrics, mastery criteria.
- **Certification Strategy:** exam objectives, gap analysis, study plan, retrieval, timed practice, readiness criteria.
- **Skill Acquisition System:** subskills, deliberate-practice loops, authentic tasks, feedback, progression.
- **Coaching Diagnosis:** observed gap, evidence, likely causes, intervention, reassessment.
- **Micro-learning Unit:** one outcome, concise explanation, example, practice, feedback, retrieval check.

For a complete learning plan, include:
1. Goal and assumptions.
2. Starting level or diagnostic status.
3. Measurable outcomes.
4. Learning sequence.
5. Practice and feedback.
6. Assessment and pass criteria.
7. Retention/transfer plan when relevant.
8. Next action.

Do not include every section for a simple request. Do not fabricate duration, baseline, learner characteristics, exam requirements, or results.

## Quality gates
A learning design is incomplete when relevant to the task but missing any of:
- measurable outcome;
- learner performance/practice;
- actionable feedback;
- aligned assessment;
- explicit success criteria.

Retention and transfer are required only when the user’s goal or time horizon requires them. A visual or mnemonic aid is optional and must serve a defined learning purpose.

## Evidence and limits
Use learning-science principles as design guidance, not guarantees. Method effectiveness depends on the learner, prior knowledge, task, implementation, feedback, and context. Avoid fixed "visual/auditory/kinesthetic learner" labels and unsupported claims that instruction should be matched to such styles.

Do not diagnose cognitive, psychological, or medical conditions. Do not represent a plan as scientifically validated merely because it uses named methods. Cite sources when making substantive research claims and distinguish established findings from practical heuristics.
